\subsection{EEG Foundation models}
% TODO Grammar check but content OK
Foundation models are large-scale machine learning models trained on vast datasets.
The ones that adopt self-supervised learning techniques typically learn general latent representations.
These models are commonly used as backbones for downstream tasks.
There have been various proposed models for EEG representation learning.
These, most times, suffer from limitations that are directly linked to the size and task specificity of their datasets.

For this reason they are large-scale when relative to the domain rather than a fixed scale.
The typical size of an EEG signals based dataset is in the order of 10²-10³ trials.
If these are to be compared to the sizes of vision/audio where samples can be of the order of the million it is clear
that the data is a strong limitation when designing robust EEG foundation models.

EEG measurements are very experiment-dependent and subject-specific.
Unless a novel approach is able to abstract the subject-specific encoding inside the data, most solutions will encode these patterns in the learnt representations.
Most often in the field evaluations are performed either within or cross subject.
When working within subject the tasks are often simpler and inflated as the biometric signatures of the participants is already known.
Cross-subject are instead harder and closer to a real use of the final model.

Beyond this, EEG experiments can vary much between different datasets.
The measurement layout used for \textit{AMIGOS}\cite{8554112} with the \textit{Emotiv EPOC Neuroheadset}, for example only tracks some
of the electrodes tracked by \textit{DEAP}\cite{5871728} and its \textit{BioSemi ActiveTwo EEG recording system}.
It also runs at a different sampling rate.
This all leads to severe domain shifts carros datasets.
Thus, the cross-dataset compatibility tends to be lower than for other common tasks such as vision and audio.
All these things considered, models trained on these datasets tend to be too correlated to the subjects and experimental setups of the data gathering process.

Another non-trivial notion to consider is that EEG signals have no natural token structure.
They derive from a continuous waveform arbitrarily segmented and this leads to limitations in their tokenization.
Relevant patterns can be observed over different temporal scales, ranging from milliseconds, seconds to even minutes.
This limits the way to approach the construction of natural embedding models, and while the task seems structurally closer
to audio than other modalities EEG lacks a known discrete structure, which in audio is represented by latent discrete units (e.g., phonemes in speech).
This leads to model-dependent and non-canonical tokenization.


Amongst these, like \textit{Biosignal Transformer for Cross-data Learning in the Wild} (BIOT) some adopt the temporal transformer paradigm where EEG are treated as a sequence.
It learns temporal dependencies by attending over different time steps in an attention mechanism.
These tend to handle the electrode geometry in a very limited way.
BIOT for example flattens the different EEG channels thus limiting the spatial correlation of the input signals.
It is known that the spatial relations of the electrodes in many processes is relevant and non-random.
For this reason a stronger spatial awareness is thought to lead to stronger results.

With this idea in mind other models integrate the spatial awareness of the electrodes on the scalp directly via graph modeling or other
novel approaches such as the \textit{criss-cross transformer} of the \textit{CBraMod}.

\textit{CBraMod} representations are in fact not tied to the single electrode position but to the relative space the nodes occupy.
This way the model learns geometry-aware representations of the data reducing the bias coming from the measurement setup.
But it is not able to overcome the data limitations on its own due to the lack of large-scale multi-dataset training.

So, despite advances regarding spatial modeling, current EEG foundation models are still limited by dataset scale,
inter-subject variability, and predominant unimodal training paradigms.

\subsection{Multimodal Contrastive Models}
% TODO parla di Biot
Gathering more data is often a costly and difficult task.
This motivates researchers to explore ways to exploit information from all available sources.

Such approaches have been widely adopted in many fields.
\textit{Flamingo}\cite{alayrac2022flamingovisuallanguagemodel} by Google DeepMind is an application that partially inspired this work.
It is a Visual-Language model that uses two frozen backbones for image and text generation.
It leverages information given by the visual encoder to enrich the textual representations.
The model requires the two data sources to be interleaved to encode the spatio-temporal information that derives from the visual encoder inside the corresponding segments of text.
The visual tokens generated by the corresponding backbone are used to condition the text with a \textit{cross-attention} mechanism\cite{vaswani2023attentionneed}.
This is applied in an interleaved way between the layers defined inside the frozen LM and the new architecture.

This way, the model is able to preserve the capabilities of its \textit{LM} while injecting the multimodal information progressively.

While Flamingo represents a fusion-based approach, many multimodal systems instead rely on contrastive objectives to align representations across modalities.

Contrastive fusion models commonly adopt loss functions inspired by the CLIP\cite{radford2021learningtransferablevisualmodels} model objective.
While CLIP itself is not a fusion model, its loss solves a problem in the representation learning domain that transfers well over different modalities.
\textit{InfoNCE} frames unsupervised learning as an instance discrimination problem where a model learns to separate positive pairs from a set of negatives.
In the context of multimodal learning the pool of samples is built by the different modalities vectors.
A similarity matrix is then constructed on these where the diagonal represents the positives and off-diagonal act as negatives.
The CLIP formulation made the objective symmetric by applying it in both directions.
This enforces alignment between representations from different modalities.

Many fusion models often use contrastive losses for pretraining or alignment.

The \textit{Momentum Contrast (MoCo)}\cite{he2020momentumcontrastunsupervisedvisual} model proposed a solution to solve limitations posed by CLIP style losses and contrastive learning.
It builds a dynamic dictionary with a queue and a moving-averaged encoder.
It reduces the memory load of the model during training by generating a queue of computed samples to run the contrastive loss on.
This way, when in constrained environments, the \textit{batch size} limits can be mitigated by having a representation proxy of the past processed samples.
To avoid the representations from drifting between iterations the framework makes use of a moving average instance of the model.

representation learning remain limited, particularly under data scarce conditions.

\subsection{Knowledge Distillation}
\textit{Knowledge Distillation (KD)} is a simple and widely adopted technique.
Its idea is to leverage a teacher in a process where its learnt representations are mimicked by a target student.
Its most common use case is for model compression, reducing the parameter count and computational cost.

Distillation as a framework can be applied at different points of a model's architecture.
It can also happen in a recursive fashion as self-distillation.

\textit{KD} introduces an auxiliary signal that guides the optimization process during training, acting as a secondary loss term.
This way the information contained in the teacher is transferred indirectly to the student.
The student model is trained to approximate the teacher’s representations, thereby transferring higher-level structural information into a more constrained architecture.
For the distillation to work, it is necessary that both models process aligned data, or else alignment would be pointless.
VATE\cite{10.1007/978-3-031-91575-8_13}, a multimodal dataset for video, audio and text in the affective computing domain, provides a suitable teacher source for cross-modal distillation into EEG representations.

The technique in cross-modal applications is being explored more and more in many contexts.
However, its application to EEG representation learning is still limited even when multimodal data are available.
For this reason, we investigate cross-modal distillation in the EEG domain.


Taken together the integration of these techniques is not sufficiently explored yet.
